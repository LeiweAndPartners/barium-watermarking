# Leiwe & Partners Writing Style Guide

*Derived from "The EU AI Act: I read it so you don't have to" (whitepaper) and the fullhuman draft of the watermarking series. Use this for all posts in the Digital Watermarking series.*

## Voice: the expert who did the reading

Position yourself as someone who read the source material so the reader doesn't have to, then distills it with judgment, not neutrality.

- "I read it so you don't have to"
- "I will summarise my take home findings here"
- "This is another way of asking 'do I need to watermark my AI content?'"

## Direct address to a specific reader

Second-person "you", aimed at builders, developers, and founders. Practical stakes: money, liability, reach.

- "you might think you've also outsourced your compliance responsibilities"
- "your software cannot be a total black box"
- "Feel free to try, I just don't expect you'll succeed in your application."

## Problem framing: the specific before the general

Open with the moment the problem happens to *this* reader, then zoom out to the rule. The reader's own stakes (money, liability, reach, a deadline) should land in the first two paragraphs, not wait for a later section.

- News events are a good hook, but the reader must see themselves in the problem, not just other people ("Last week, Claude users started cancelling" describes *their* anger; "your product ships in November and 2 December is the deadline" is *your* decision)
- If a paragraph could start "compliance is complex," rewrite it to start with the moment it becomes complex
- Already done well in places: "a hacky vibe-coded prototype", "under $50 in API queries"

## Opinionated, with "my take" moments

Take positions and label them as yours. Comfortable with "probably" and "my take" for judgment calls.

- "my take is marketing is commercial speech"
- "I believe this has been intentionally left broad"
- "Honestly, watermarking is not unbreakable."

## Wry humour, used sparingly

Asides and light jokes that land because they're rare.

- "(one can but dream)"
- "Essentially no Minority Report systems"
- "Cue a whole load of LinkedIn and Reddit posts complaining"
- "a hacky vibe-coded prototype"

## Sentence craft: short + long, colons for setup-payoff

Punchy sentences land next to longer explanatory ones. Colons set up the payoff.

- "The short answer is... it depends."
- "In short, if the output of your AI system or model is consumed in the EU you are bound by the Act."
- "The mark is a receipt, not a brand."

## Memorable framing lines

One-liners that carry the argument. These are the signature.

- "providers mark, deployers disclose"
- "label it or own it"
- "Compliance as a Shibboleth"
- "The Myth of Geographic Immunity"

## Metaphors: authored by the writer

Metaphors and signature lines are voice markers, so they come from the writer, not the assistant. This is a working rule, not a preference.

- One metaphor per post, chosen by the writer
- The assistant flags where a metaphor would strengthen the post and asks the writer; it never supplies the final line
- Protect a metaphor once it lands; don't stack a second one on top

## Myth-busting structure

Frame sections as myths to kill, not topics to explain.

- "Myth 1: We can just block European IP addresses"
- "Myth 2: We can just put 'Not for EU use' in the software contract"

Use the myth label only for claims people actually repeat. For every other section, keep the same logical flow (problem → evidence → verdict) without the label. The structure stays consistent; only the surface changes.

## Concrete evidence over abstraction

Named companies, real numbers, dated events. Never vague.

- "9,000+ GitHub stars", "47% less reach", "under $50 in API queries", "€35 million or 7% of global annual turnover"
- Worked examples with named jurisdictions (Hong Kong developer, Singapore client, Dublin subsidiary)

## Honest about uncertainty

Flag what the law doesn't answer. Weave in disclaimers naturally.

- "the Act never defines 'matters of public interest'"
- "(and I am not a lawyer so don't take this as legal advice)"

## Language mechanics

- **UK English**: summarise, penalise, authorised, labelling, organisation, recognise
- **Contractions, freely**: don't, you're, it's, isn't
- **Em-dashes, rarely**: prefer commas, colons, parentheses, or full stops. Allow one only when the alternative is genuinely worse (a comma-dense parenthetical, a dramatic interruption), never more than one or two per post. Table cells are exempt: "—" is the standard placeholder for "not applicable".
- **Bold lead-ins used sparingly**: mostly for key terms, not every sentence

## What to avoid (the LLM tells)

- The "**Bold lead-in:** explanation" pattern on every point (very ChatGPT rhythm)
- Balanced two-sided hedging everywhere ("on one hand... on the other")
- Generic transitions ("In conclusion", "Furthermore", "Moreover")
- Listicle-itis: numbered lists only when there's a real sequence
- Em-dash as the default connector (rare, deliberate exceptions only)
- "But here's the thing", "Let's be honest", and "It's worth noting that" openers
